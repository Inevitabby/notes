title: "Camera Formats and Analog Film"
---

The physical size of the film negative or digital sensor fundamentally dictates the look, feel, and working process of the resulting photograph. Photographers often choose their format based on the level of clarity, flexibility, and psychological engagement they want with their subjects.

# 35mm and Small Formats

The smallest widely adopted analog format fundamentally changed the speed and portability of photography.

**35mm Film**: A small, highly portable film format originally established for cinema but adapted for rapid, candid still photography.
- Allowed photographers to shoot discretely on the street.
- Popularized by early compact cameras like the Leica.[^leica]

[^leica]: Introduced in the 1920s, the Leica camera was revolutionary because it was small enough to fit in a pocket, allowed for quick succession shooting, and didn't require a tripod, thereby birthing modern photojournalism and street photography.

# Medium Format and Toy Cameras

Medium format offers a physically larger negative than 35mm, providing greater resolution while still maintaining a degree of handheld portability. It requires less technical setup than large format. 

**Toy Cameras**: Inexpensive plastic cameras (such as the Holga and Diana) that shoot medium format film.
- Known for their low-fidelity aesthetic, characterized by cheap plastic lenses that heavily blur the edges of the frame.
- Susceptible to accidental light leaks, adding unpredictable artifacts to the image.

**Light Leaks**: Distinctive, unpredictable streaks of light that mark the film because a camera's plastic body is imperfect and allows outside light to seep in.

## Notable Medium Format Approaches

Photographers utilize the square framing and deliberate pace of medium format for both street and fine art applications.

> **Example**: Diane Arbus
>
> Arbus left the superficiality of fashion photography, taking a twin-lens reflex medium-format camera onto the streets. Unlike a 35mm camera held to the eye, a medium format camera is usually held at the waist. This allowed Arbus to look her subjects directly in the eye and build relationships while shooting, fundamentally changing the psychological dynamic of her portraits of marginalized individuals.

# Large Format

Choosing large format prioritizes extreme clarity, print flexibility, and a slow, deliberate process over convenience. Using a large format camera completely changes the dynamic between photographer and subject. 

**Large Format**: Cameras that utilize massive individual sheets of film (e.g., 4x5, 8x10, or 20x24 inches).
- The photographer must hide under a dark cloth to view the upside-down projection on the ground glass.[^ground_glass]
- Creates an intimidating but deeply collaborative atmosphere. 
- The resulting detail is described as "unforgiving."

[^ground_glass]: Unlike modern viewfinders, large format cameras project the image onto a piece of frosted glass at the rear of the camera. Because there are no internal mirrors to correct the optics, the image appears entirely upside down and backwards to the photographer.

## Notable Large Format Approaches

The unwieldy nature of large format requires specific methodological approaches, whether traversing landscapes or building massive sets.

> **Example**: Historical and Environmental Portraiture
>
> William Henry Jackson shot massive 20x24" plates of the Wild West in the 1880s using donkeys to haul gear, proving that the grandeur of the West was real to skeptical East Coast citizens. 
> 
> Alec Soth traveled the Mississippi River with an 8x10 camera, using the slow, cumbersome device as a conversation starter; setting up the camera took so long that it acted as a shield for his shyness, allowing his subjects to relax and collaborate in the portrait process.
> 
> Eva O'Leary shoots 8x10 portraits of adolescent girls through a one-way mirror, shifting the power dynamic of the gaze.
>

> **Example**: Conceptual and Cinematic Staging
>
> Edward Weston used an 8x10 camera to shoot fine art still lifes (like *Pepper #30*) using incredibly small apertures (like f/64) and natural light, resulting in exposures that lasted up to 6 hours to achieve perfect sharpness from front to back. 
>
> Gregory Crewdson creates cinematic setups with massive lighting crews, Hollywood actors, and street closures to tell a single, hyper-detailed story in a single large-format frame.
