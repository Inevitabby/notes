title: "Contemporary Landscape: Typologies and Non-Representational Art"
---

In the last 50–70 years, fine art landscape photography shifted as artists recognized the limitations of straight, modernist photography. They sought more complex, conceptual ways to describe the landscape, leading to highly structured and referential works.

# Typologies and Classification

Some contemporary photographers treat the landscape as a scientific archive, creating grids of related structures.

**Typology**: An artwork or series of artworks illustrating a strict classification system or set of types.[^typology_origin]
- Relies on repetition and identical visual framing.
- Allows viewers to easily compare minute architectural differences.

[^typology_origin]: The term is borrowed from archaeology and biology, where specimens are rigidly sorted by physical characteristics to understand evolutionary or structural changes over time.

> **Example**: Hilla and Bernd Becher
>
> The Bechers, a German husband-and-wife duo, spent decades driving around Europe in a van. They created massive grid collages of similar industrial architectural structures (water towers, blast furnaces, grain silos). To ensure the structures could be objectively compared, they photographed them exclusively on overcast days (to eliminate shadows) from the exact same perspective and distance.

# Materiality and Process Interventions

Certain conceptual artists physically incorporate the landscape into the photographic medium itself.

**Process Intervention**: A technique where the artist uses elements of the physical landscape to actively degrade or construct the final photographic print.
- Connects the image inextricably to the physical space it depicts.

> **Example**: Abelardo Morell and Mathew Brandt
>
> Abelardo Morell blacks out the windows of a room, leaving a small hole to turn the room into a giant camera obscura. He projects the outside landscape (like the Golden Gate Bridge) onto the interior walls or bedsheets, then photographs the projection, creating a textured overlay. 
> 
> Mathew Brandt goes a step further by physically degrading his C-prints[^c_print] using materials from the landscape itself. He submerges photos of a lake into water collected from that exact lake, causing the emulsion to bubble and decay. For photos of a demolished high school, he mixed dust from the actual demolition site into the chemical emulsion.
>

[^c_print]: A chromogenic print (C-print) is the standard darkroom process for printing analog color photographs. The paper contains multiple layers of silver salts and color dyes that are highly susceptible to chemical and physical degradation.

# The Landscape of Capitalism and the Digital Age

Contemporary artists heavily explore how consumerism, globalization, and the internet have altered our perception of physical space.

**Digital and Capitalist Landscapes**: Artworks that highlight how human experience has been degraded or mediated by corporate spaces and digital interfaces.
- Frequently utilizes digital manipulation, screen scraping,[^screen_scraping] or found online imagery.

[^screen_scraping]: The automated extraction of data or imagery from websites. Artists use this to aggregate thousands of public images to make a point about collective visual culture.

> **Example**: Hyper-real Capitalism
>
> Andreas Gursky creates massive (often 10-foot-wide), hyper-real landscapes. By digitally stitching together multiple large-format frames, he creates impossibly vast, perfectly sharp interiors of 99-cent stores or endless Amazon fulfillment centers. The sheer scale and uniform detail overwhelm the viewer, mirroring the endless cycle of global consumerism.
>

> **Example**: Digital Mediation and Surveillance
>
> Doug Rickard never left his home; he used Google Street View to travel through disenfranchised American cities. By photographing his computer screen with a tripod, he captured a detached, pixelated, apocalyptic vision of America entirely mediated by Google's surveillance cameras. 
> 
> Penelope Umbrico created massive gallery installations by searching the word "Sunset" on Flickr and downloading thousands of results. By compiling them into a massive grid, she pointed out how the most intimate, romantic experiences in nature have been reduced to repetitive, licensed visual clichés.
