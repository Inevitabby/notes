title: "Documentary Photography and the Archive"
---

Documentary photography is a form of non-fiction storytelling invented during the modernist era. It draws heavily on sociology and anthropology to record how the world looks, often with a dedicated social, historical, or environmental focus.

# The Camera as an Index

One major branch of documentary photography treats the camera as an objective tool for methodical indexing, cataloguing, and historical preservation.[^indexical_medium]

[^indexical_medium]: In semiotics, a photograph is considered an "indexical" sign—meaning it has a direct, physical footprint linking it to reality, because light from the actual subject physically altered the chemistry of the film.

**Archival Documentation**: The practice of using the camera to methodically catalogue a location, object, or culture before it disappears or changes.
- Relies on consistency and high volume.

**Rephotographic Survey**: A specific documentary methodology where a photographer returns to the exact location[^rephotography_alignment] of a historical photo and takes a new image to document the passage of time and physical change.

[^rephotography_alignment]: This often involves painstaking research to find the exact tripod holes, lens focal length, and time of day used by the original photographer decades earlier to make the comparison visually accurate.

> **Example**: Eugene Atget
>
> An archivist of Paris in the early 1900s. As the mayor began modernizing Paris by knocking down old neighborhoods and replacing narrow wooden streets with wide concrete avenues, Atget methodically documented the vanishing city. His bulky 8x10 camera required long exposures, resulting in empty streets or ghostly, blurred figures, making the documentation of a place on the precipice of erasure foundational to the documentary genre.

> **Example**: Rephotography in Practice
>
> Patrick Nagatani undertook a massive project to photograph the desolate, empty landscapes where Japanese internment camps used to stand, contrasting the violent history of the space with its current quiet emptiness. Similarly, The Professor (Turner) used rephotography to document the decommissioned Fort MacArthur Air Force Base, tracking how military architecture rots and changes when abandoned to time.

# Social and Environmental Documentary

Also known as "concerned photography," this branch moves beyond mere cataloging to advocate for awareness and change.

**Concerned Photography**: Documentary work that highlights specific political, social, or environmental realities to an audience, often to provoke empathy or outrage.[^advocacy_journalism]
- Projects can take years or decades to complete.

[^advocacy_journalism]: Unlike daily photojournalism, which theoretically strives for neutral objectivity, concerned documentary photography openly takes a stance and advocates for the subjects it portrays.

> **Example**: Social Realities
>
> Luz Mila Carmona spearheaded the La Boquilla Project, an initiative where local community leaders in Colombian shantytowns were given point-and-shoot cameras. This allowed them to document their own lives authentically, bypassing the external gaze of foreign photojournalists. Jonathan Torgovnik spent years photographing and interviewing Rwandan women who raised children born of rape during the genocide, highlighting complex, invisible post-war realities.

> **Example**: Environmental Degradation
>
> Ed Burtynsky creates massive, beautiful, large-format documentary projects focusing on the horrific scale of industrial landscapes. His project *Manufactured Landscapes* explored massive Chinese factories where human labor is dwarfed by machinery; *Shipbreaking* documented monolithic ships being dismantled by hand in Bangladesh; and *Oil* was a 17-year project detailing the extraction, car-culture life cycle, and entropic, polluting end of fossil fuels.
