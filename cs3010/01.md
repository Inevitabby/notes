# Numeric Algorithms

A **numeric algorithm** solves a problem for which the answer is a number.
- These algorithms are *always* going to be dealing with a certain amount of imprecision.

> **On Imprecision**
> 
> Computers have finite resources, but some numbers (e.g., irrational numbers) have an infinite amount of digits.
> - Irrational Number: A number that can be represented as a ratio of two integers.
> 
> Even if you had every particle in the universe, you couldn't represent PI.
> 
> Imprecision can accumulate across calculations.
> 
> **Silly Fact:**
> - There are some numbers can be made rational by changing their base *(e.g., 1/3 is 0.1 in base 3)*
> - You can always make the base whatever the denominator is to avoid that problem.

## Numeric V.S. Symbolic

Algorithms can be split in 2 categories:
- Numeric: Algorithms that can solve problems that the answer for which is a number(s).
- Symbolic: Algorithms where the answer is not a number.
	* Not a "symbol", symbolic.
	* example: program that returns "Hello World" is (symbolic) sequence of characters.

> **Everything is Symbols**
> 
> You can play the game of, "a sequence of characters can be represented with numbers"; but you can make this conversion forever.
> 
> Sometimes, a function that outputs an integer can be interpretted symbollically, et cetera.

# Number Representation in General

A positional number system (aka: based on exponentiation) is defined by a set of digits, where numbers are sequences/strings.

$$
\boxed{
	\text{Let } b = ??? \text{ and } D = \{ d_0,d_1,d_2,d_3,...,d_n \}
}
$$
- A number is a sequence $n_i \in D$

> **Example**: Value of a Number
> 
> $$
> n_4 n_3 n_2 n_1 = 
> n_1 \times b^0 +
> n_2 \times b^1 +
> n_3 \times b^2 + 
> n_4 \times b^3 
> $$
> 
> With a base 10 number:
> 
> $$
> 1^3 9^2 3^1 7^0 =
> 7 \times 10^0 + 
> 3 \times 10^1 + 
> 9 \times 10^2 + 
> 1 \times 10^3
> $$
> 
> With a base 2 number:
> 
> $$
> 1^3 1^2 0^1 1^0 =
> 1 \times 2^0 + 
> 0 \times 2^1 + 
> 1 \times 2^2 + 
> 1 \times 2^3
> $$

# Number Representation in Computers

Modern computers represent numbers in binary ($b=2, D = \{0,1\}$).

Why? Because it's easier in hardware.
- The tradeoff for this simplicity is that the strings are longer (more memory).

> **Example**: Decimal to Binary
> 
> Let's convert 633 to binary with successive division.
> - As a refresher, this is just about dividing by two a bunch and noting remainder.
> - Remember: Don't round anything, always truncate.
> 
> | 633 | 1 |
> |-----|---|
> | 316 | 0 |
> | 115 | 0 |
> | 79  | 1 |
> | 39  | 1 |
> | 19  | 1 |
> | 9   | 1 |
> | 4   | 0 |
> | 2   | 0 |
> | 1   | 1 |
> | 0   |   |
> 
> We read the number from the bottom-up, so the answer is:
> 
> `1001111001`
