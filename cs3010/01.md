# Numeric Algorithms

A **numeric algorithm** solves a problem for which the answer is a number.
- These algorithms are *always* going to be dealing with a certain amount of imprecision.

> **On Imprecision**
> 
> Computers have finite resources, but some numbers (e.g., irrational numbers) have an infinite amount of digits.
> - Irrational Number: A number that can be represented as a ratio of two integers.
> 
> Even if you had every particle in the universe, you couldn't represent PI.
> 
> Imprecision can accumulate across calculations.
> 
> **Silly Fact:**
> - There are some numbers can be made rational by changing their base *(e.g., 1/3 is 0.1 in base 3)*
> - You can always make the base whatever the denominator is to avoid that problem.

## Numeric V.S. Symbolic

Algorithms can be split in 2 categories:
- Numeric: Algorithms that can solve problems that the answer for which is a number(s).
- Symbolic: Algorithms where the answer is not a number.
	* Not a "symbol", symbolic.
	* example: program that returns "Hello World" is (symbolic) sequence of characters.

> **Everything is Symbols**
> 
> You can play the game of, "a sequence of characters can be represented with numbers"; but you can make this conversion forever.
> 
> Sometimes, a function that outputs an integer can be interpretted symbollically, et cetera.

# Number Representation in General

A positional number system (aka: based on exponentiation) is defined by a set of digits, where numbers are sequences/strings.

$$
\boxed{
	\text{Let } b = ??? \text{ and } D = \{ d_0,d_1,d_2,d_3,...,d_n \}
}
$$
- A number is a sequence $n_i \in D$

> **Example**: Value of a Number
> 
> $$
> n_4 n_3 n_2 n_1 = 
> n_1 \times b^0 +
> n_2 \times b^1 +
> n_3 \times b^2 + 
> n_4 \times b^3 
> $$
> 
> With a base 10 number:
> 
> $$
> 1^3 9^2 3^1 7^0 =
> 7 \times 10^0 + 
> 3 \times 10^1 + 
> 9 \times 10^2 + 
> 1 \times 10^3
> $$
> 
> With a base 2 number:
> 
> $$
> 1^3 1^2 0^1 1^0 =
> 1 \times 2^0 + 
> 0 \times 2^1 + 
> 1 \times 2^2 + 
> 1 \times 2^3
> $$

# Number Representation in Computers

Modern computers represent numbers in binary ($b=2, D = \{0,1\}$).

Why? Because it's easier in hardware.
- The tradeoff for this simplicity is that the strings are longer (more memory).

> **Example**: Decimal to Binary
> 
> Let's convert 633 to binary with successive division.
> - As a refresher, this is just about dividing by two a bunch and noting remainder.
> - Remember: Don't round anything, always truncate.
> 
> | 633 | 1 |
> |-----|---|
> | 316 | 0 |
> | 115 | 0 |
> | 79  | 1 |
> | 39  | 1 |
> | 19  | 1 |
> | 9   | 1 |
> | 4   | 0 |
> | 2   | 0 |
> | 1   | 1 |
> | 0   |   |
> 
> We read the number from the bottom-up, so the answer is:
> 
> `1001111001`

# Significance 

Suppose the expansion of the decimal number 9378.

$$
9^4 + 2^3 7^2 8^1 2^0
$$
- The **significance** of a digit is the power.
	* e.g., 9's significance in this example is 4
- The most significant digit is 9.
- The least significance digit is 2.
	* It contributes very little to the total value.
	* Most of it's effect is in simply taking space and pushing the other numbers up.

## Loss of Significance

**Loss of Significance**: We can incur a loss of significance when storing numbers or doing arithetic.

$$
\boxed{
	\text{Absolute Error: } AE = |X - X_s|
} \\~\\
\boxed{
	\text{Relative Error: } RE = \left| 
		\frac{X - X_s}{X}
	\right|
} \\
\small
\textit{where $X$ is the original value, and} \\
\textit{where $X_s$ is the stored/approximated value}
$$

> **Example**: Loss of Significance
> 
> Suppose a decimal computer than can numbers with 5 significant digits.
> 
> Let's say we want to store some samples and store them in the computer.
> 
> | Sample (i) | Value      |
> |------------|------------|
> | 1          | 15.0783073 |
> 
> Q: The value uses 9 significant digits, how can we store it in the computer?
> 
> A: We can truncate the number, dropping the least significant bits.
> - For sample 1, this means storing only `15.078`.
> - This incurs a loss of significance with an absolute error of `0.0003073`, or relative error of `0.002038%`

> **Remember**: There are <!-- cathedrals everywhere for those with the eyes to see --> errors everywhere.
> - Even instruments can have error.

# Floating Point Numbers

**Floating-Point**: A number with a point in it.

## Scientific Notation

Suppose the decimal floating-point `34.75601`.

We can represent it in **scientific notation**.
- **How**: Move the point until only one digit is on the left of the point, then multiply the number by $10$ raised to a power equal to the number of places we moved the point.
	* The $10$ is the base.
- **Why**: Every time we move the decimal left is equivalent to dividing by 10 (the base). 
	- We could use a negative exponent to move the point right.

We can do the same for binary numbers.

> **Example**: Scientific notation for binary floating-point
> 
> **Q**: Find the scientific notation of the binary number 101.1011
> 
> **A**: $1.011011_2 \times 10_2^{2_{10}}$
> - Remember, we are multiplying by the base, which is 2.

> **Note**: One interesting property of binary floating-points in scientific notation is the MSB has to be 1 (or else it wouldn't be the MSB)
