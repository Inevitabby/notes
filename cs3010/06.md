# Trapezoid Method

Deriving ???

Q: 

$$
f(x) = x^2 + 3x + 1
$$

$$
x_0 = 1, x_1 = 2
$$

Find $\int f(x)dx$ from 1 to 2.

A:

**it1**:

$$
A_0 = \left[ 
	\frac{f(1) + f(2)}{1}
\right] (1) = 8
$$

**it2**: 

$$
A_1 = \left[ 
	\frac{f(1) + f(1.5)}{2}
\right] (0.5) = 3.1875
$$

$$
A_2 = \left[ 
	\frac{f(1.5) + f(2)}{2}
\right] (0.5) = 4.6875
$$

---

$$
A_1 + A_2 = 7.875
$$

**it3**:

$$
A_1 = \left[ 
	\frac{f(1) + f(1.25)}{2}
\right] (0.25) = 1.4140625
$$

$$
A_2 = \left[ 
	\frac{f(1.5) + f(1.75)}{2}
\right] (0.25) = 2.1328125
$$

$$
A_3 = \left[ 
	\frac{f(1.75) + f(2)}{2}
\right] (0.25) = 2.5390625
$$

$$
A_1 + A_2 + A_3 = 7.8437
$$

> Note — This will never hit the solution, unless you do this $\infin$ times.

# Romberg's Method

~~Deriving~~

$$
h_i = \frac{h}{2^i}
$$

$h=b-a$

Base:

$$
R(0,0)=\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2} (h_0)
$$

???:

$$
R(i,0)=\frac{R(i-1,0)}{2} (h_i)\sum_{k=1}^{2^i -1} f(a+(2k-1)h_i)
$$

$$
R(i,j)=R(i,j-1) + \frac{R(i,j-1) - R(i-1, j-1)}{4^j - 1}
$$

---

Q: 

$$
f(x) = x^2 + 3x + 1
$$

$$
x_0 = 1, x_1 = 2
$$

Find $\int f(x)dx$ from 1 to 2.

A:

**it1**: h=1

$$
R(0,0)=\frac{f(1)+f(2)}{2} (1)\\
= 8
$$

**it2**: h=0.5

$$
R(1,0) = \frac{R(0,0)}{2} + (0.5) \sum_{k=1}^{1} f(1 + 0.5) \\
= \frac{8}{2} - (0.5)f(1.5) \\
= 7.875
$$

> **Notice** — We got the same number as last time, but didn't need to plug into the function four times.

$$
R(1,1) = R(1,0) + \frac{R(1,0) - R(0,0)}{3} \\
= 7.875 + \frac{7.875 - 8}{3} \\
= 7.8\overline{3}
$$

We've converged to the actual value of the integral, something which was impossible with the trapezoid method.
- If we didn't know we converged and did another iteration:

**it3**: h=0.25

$$
R(2,0) = \frac{R(1,0)}{2} + (0.25)(f(1 + 0.25) + f(1 + (3)(0.25))) \\
= \frac{7.875}{2} + (0.25)(f(1.25) + f(1.75)) \\
= 7.84375
$$

> While the work increased, it's way less than the 8 plug-ins we did in trapezoidal.

$$
R(2,1) = 7.84375 + \frac{7.84375 - 7.875}{3} \\
= 7.8\overline{3}
$$

$$
R(2,2) = 7.8\overline{3} + \frac{7.8\overline{3} - 7.8\overline{3}}{3} \\
= 7.8\overline{3}
$$

As you can see, we'll just continue to get the same answer over-and-over now.

> **Professor's Note** — For this algorithm, students *always* falter with the summation.
> - Make sure you know how to expand the summation.
> - Especially won't mess up the powers of k
