# Four Categories of AI

## 1. Acting Humanly

Turing Test: Test of machine's ability to exhibit intelligent behaviour equivalent to a human's.
- Proposed by Alan Turing in 1950

> **Aside**: Human Behavior v.s. Intelligent Behavior, and more limitations
> 
> The Turing Test has many weaknesses.
> - Interrogators may be naive/unsuspecting (e.g., random online comments),
> - Human behavior isn't necessarily intelligent (e.g., the first Loebner winner imitated human typing errors),
> - It only measures external behavior (e.g., are you *actually* thinking, or simulating thinking),
> - It may be irrelevant (e.g., aeronautical engineering texts don't try to make machines that fly exactly like pigeons to fool other pigeons)

> **Aside**: Turing Trap 
> 
> **Turing Trap**: A focus on imitation over augmentation of humans means your research is just about replacing humans, which drives down worker wages and income.
> - This *directly* removes economic and political power from workers and gives it to people up the food chain.
> - In the modern day, there are strong incentives to do this to increase corporate profits (layoffs are already the first lever companies pull to please shareholders and keep stocks high)

## 2. Thinking Humanly

Cognitive Modeling: Validate by predicting and testing behavior of human subjects.
- Intertwined with scientific theories of how the brain works.
- Roughly two approaches: Cognitive Science and Cognitive Neuroscience.

## 3. Thinking Rationally

Laws of Thoughts: What *are* correct arguments and thought processes?
- It isn't easy to state informal knowledge, formally. 
- Solving problems in principle and solving problems in practice are different.

## 4. Acting Rationally

> This is the route we will pursue in this course.

Rational Agents: Something that acts (an agent) doing the right thing (rationality).
- More general than "thinking rationally", and doesn't try to imitate humans.

| Philosophy           | Logic, methods of reasoning, language, rationality    |
